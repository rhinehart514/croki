diagram language study · three organizing axes

Same living pipeline, three ways to draw it

ARiver — flow is a volume of people

The pipeline is one stream of real people. Width = how many are flowing, so conviction is literal: the spine is a fat channel, a fresh experiment is a trickle, a dead branch is a dried bed tapering to nothing. You watch the river redistribute as a bet wins. Switches are forks; the amber gate is a band the whole river crosses before it pools into an outcome.

best: volume + growth are felt viscerally · weak: exact step logic gets fuzzy at a fork
BTransit — channels are lines, steps are stops

Borrow the one diagram humans already read at huge complexity: the subway map. Each channel is a colored line, each step a stop, and switches are interchange stations where lines meet and logic routes you. A live experiment is a dashed “under construction” extension; a retired step is a hollow closed station. Scales to dozens of channels without ever becoming a hairball — that’s the whole reason transit maps exist.

best: legible at extreme scale, iconic · weak: volume + “happening now” are harder to feel
CTimeline — the x-axis is time, the program grows on it

Don’t lay it out by topology at all — lay it out by when. The spine is a thick track running across the weeks. Every experiment is a branch that lifts off the spine the week you added it, runs in parallel, then either merges back in (promoted — and the spine thickens) or ends in a dot (killed). The strategist’s promote/prune calls become events on the line. This draws the thing you actually said: GTM grows by adding and experimenting, over and over.

best: shows the living/accreting nature directly, scrubbable history · weak: current-state topology is implicit, not the focus
