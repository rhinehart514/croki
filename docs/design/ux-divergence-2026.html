Drover · Design direction · The ADE turn · 2026-07-15

Drover becomes an ADE.

Drover's canvas renders a living venture as a spatial world. Today it floats naked — panels overlap it, nodes collide, it leaks its own internal vocabulary at the founder, and it never adapts to the screen it's on. The thesis of this document: Drover becomes an Agent Development Environment (ADE) with the venture canvas as its center stage — the way Letta's ADE and LangSmith Studio frame an agent workspace, held to Drover's craft bar rather than their default-gray chrome. Inside that frame, the atlas gets one spatial engine, three altitudes of depth, and a language rebuild. A completed founder-interview series (2026-07-15, five areas) settled the crew, the work loop, the gate, first run, and how Drover ships; every decision below is folded through this document, with several earlier calls overturned by later machinery checks and marked as such. This hands Opus build agents a phased plan to build it.

Audience · Opus agents (build) + Jacob (taste) ■ Built today ■ Proposed next ■ Unproven

01 — Orientation

What Drover is, and who it's for

Drover is a desktop workbench for a founder running one or more ventures alone — a one-person holding company. The promise is plain: vibe code your go-to-market. A permanent AI crew researches, drafts, and builds while the founder works alongside it; product change and reaching the market are one act; and everything that would touch the outside world — every send, publish, deploy, or dollar spent — waits for the founder's hand. The founder's job compresses to judgment: stop, continue, or release. Minutes a day, not hours. (One honest constraint from the shipping decisions below: the crew works while the app is open, not overnight — see "How Drover ships.")

The primary surface is not a dashboard. It's an infinite canvas — a pannable, zoomable spatial world (built on React Flow) that shows how the venture creates value and reaches its market before it shows the machinery underneath. Chat is the easy handle that drives the deep canvas. The signature moment Drover is built around:

The frame, as of 2026-07-15

That canvas now lives inside an Agent Development Environment — an ADE. Not a naked canvas with panels floating over it, but IDE-grade chrome around the center stage, the way Letta's ADE, LangSmith Studio, or a Cursor-class editor frame their workspace: a left rail of conversation threads, the canvas as center stage holding most of the viewport, and a contextual right inspector. There is no separate activity console — chat is the console, with agent work and artifacts inline in the threads. The canvas is still the star; the ADE is the room it stands in — and, as §3 shows, the room is also the fix for the panel anarchy the current build suffers.

The signature moment

A founder types one plain sentence — "help this venture grow" — and the canvas immediately draws a complete, editable working theory of the whole venture: who it may help, how value happens, several ways to reach people, the first campaigns worth trying, and useful inward work already beginning. It appears as Drover's provisional reading, not a plan to approve. Real work and returned evidence revise it. Only an act that would touch the world waits for the founder.

The concept set — exactly six, settled 2026-07-15

Everything the founder sees is built from six nouns and no more: venture (the business), conversation (talking with the firm), effort (a piece of work the crew is attempting), teammate (a member of the crew), capability (a real reach into the world, like Gmail or a product repo), and record (the complete run history behind any artifact or event). Two concepts the interview series had floated were folded back in by a later machinery check: prepared work merged into effort — an effort simply has drafts — and trust grant merged into conversation — the firm just remembers what you told it it may do. Any component name or copy implying a seventh concept is reconciled to these six.

The success bar — founder-stated 2026-07-15

The 10/10 for this surface is "I can see everything." Total situational awareness of the firm — what's underway, what changed, what's waiting — is the criterion the founder named, ahead of leverage, embodiment, or demo appeal. Legibility wins. Every decision in this document is measured against whether it makes the whole firm more or less readable at a glance.

Two constraints govern every pixel. Desktop only — phone and tablet are not targets, and nothing here should be judged for mobile. Desktop-only is now absolute: the localhost web build survives strictly as a dev/test harness, and the old snapshot/degraded web state is eliminated (founder-decided 2026-07-15). But the canvas must adapt dynamically across desktop resolutions (roughly 1280px to 5K); the older "design only for 1920×1080" rule is retired. And founder language is ordinary language — the interface uses the founder's own words and the concrete thing happening. Drover's internal nouns — bet, motion, fork, drifting, stage, work item — may live in storage and code as compatibility seams, but must never appear in founder-facing copy.

Division of labor Claude sessions (this document) ship design direction and executable specs under docs/design/. Opus agents build the ADE shell and canvas production code (founder-amended 2026-07-15); Codex carries production work outside it. This document is direction and plan, not an edit to the app.

How Drover ships — settled 2026-07-15

Drover is a local desktop application the founder runs on his own machine, and that shapes the whole surface. A venture's data is plain readable files on disk (the existing ~/.gtm-ide location evolves) — no database, no cloud, no Drover account. Capability connections (Gmail, Exa, Apollo, and the rest) are configured locally, and the model runs on the founder's own subscriptions (the Claude Code / Codex seam stays). The shell is Electron wrapping the existing UI; the browser build stops being a founder surface and survives only as a dev/test harness. Work runs only while the app is open — there is no background service and no cloud runner. Stated honestly, that trades away overnight work: "since you left" means "since your last session," not work done while the app was closed. Distribution is open source with a paid layer later; update mechanics are deferred.

Who operates this

The design center is the solo founder — Jacob's own workflow, running one or more ventures alone. Every default in this document optimizes for that: one person, minutes a day, the composer and the stage as the whole job. That does not change.

Proposed · unproven

But the ADE frame should not structurally preclude other operator types later. Two are plausible without redefining the product: a collaborator who can answer the world-touching acts that are waiting but doesn't reshape the venture, and a viewer who walks the atlas read-only to understand a venture (a prospective buyer, an advisor). This constrains the shell in two concrete ways: releasing an act must be a permission boundary, not just a UI state — the wall's host-issued capability already models this, so the shell honors it rather than inventing it; and the atlas needs a genuine read-only mode where pan, zoom, and altitude all work but nothing mutates. Both are marked unproven in §9. The point is a design constraint now — don't hard-wire "the one operator is the one owner" into the shell — not a feature to build.

What follows: the current UX fails structurally (§2); the committed direction is the ADE shell plus three composed moves inside it (§3); each founder-facing surface — the shell's conversation rail and inspector included — gets a redesign and a state grid (§4); the internal vocabulary gets translated to ordinary language (§5); the frontier packages that make it buildable are named with their status (§6); the exploration surface records what the founder interview settled and what still needs mockup rounds (§7); a phased plan hands the build agents shippable slices (§8); and the honest open risks are stated plainly (§9).

02 — Audit

The failure is structural, not aesthetic

Audited live on 2026-07-15 against localhost:4317 in Chrome DevTools, verified at both 1920×1080 and 1440×900. The visual foundations are already good — a warm paper background, Geist type, muted greens, calm cards. The problem is underneath: the atlas is placed by hand, so it collides, clips, and never re-composes for the screen it's on. These are not polish items. Below roughly 3/10, none of them are.

Conversation, Outline, and Settings can all be open at once, stacked, covering ~60% of the canvas. Opening one never closes or re-fits the others, and the canvas never reclaims space when a panel takes it.

No resolution adaptation.

Node positions are identical absolute coordinates at 1440×900 as at 1920×1080. The "Whole venture" fit fills the full viewport, ignoring the panels sitting on top of it — so "fitted" content hides under the chrome.

root cause in source: atlasLayout.ts hard-codes a COLUMNS map (x from 40 to 3040) and an OTHER_MOTION_Y = [80, 530, -140, 750]; AtlasCanvas.tsx:106 sets fitView={false}.

07

A degraded state colors the whole session as broken.

A red "Desktop host required" alarm sits in the header, and the same message repeats in three places. A normal snapshot view reads as a system failure.

08

The venture picker uses one quadrant of the screen.

A half-empty two-column page where the firm should feel like a place. The console shows repeated 400s and one 503 during the session.

Root cause — two parts The canvas has no frame. Findings 5–7 are what happens without a shell: panels have nowhere to dock, so they float and stack; the degraded message has no home, so it's stamped everywhere. The canvas is used naively. Findings 1–4 and 6 trace to React Flow 12 (@xyflow/react 12.11.0) being installed but hand-driven — absolute positions, no auto-layout, fitView disabled, no zoom-level rendering, no minimap. Both fixes are already in the dependency tree. The direction is to give the canvas an environment and use React Flow properly, not to replace either.

03 — The direction

The ADE shell, and three moves inside it

One frame and three moves, and they compose. The ADE shell (Move 1) is the room: it ends the panel anarchy and gives the canvas a viewport it can measure. Inside it, the spatial engine (Move 2) makes the atlas place itself, semantic altitude (Move 3) turns zoom into depth, and the language and component rebuild (Move 4) runs across all of it. Build them in order — nothing inside the frame is worth polishing until the frame exists.

Move 1

The ADE shell

The frame. The fix for panel anarchy, and the viewport the canvas measures against.

Today the conversation, outline, and settings panels float over the canvas and stack on each other (audit findings 5–6). The fix is not better overlay management — it's to stop overlaying. Give Drover the shell every serious agent development environment already has: a persistent docked workbench with the canvas as its center stage. This is prior art, not invention. Letta's ADE frames an agent's memory and tools around a working canvas; LangSmith Studio and Retool both put a graph or app on center stage with a left nav rail, a docked right inspector, and a composer docked to the stage rather than floating over it. Drover takes that proven anatomy and holds it to its own craft bar — warm paper, not the default gray these tools ship.

  • Left rail — the conversation. The one surface that earns permanent residence in the rail. It holds one conversation with the firm — not a thread list. The earlier "one main dialogue plus a branching effort thread each" model was superseded by the density correction: keep a single conversation until volume genuinely hurts, then split, rather than fragmenting from the start. Ventures, crew, and the gate do not live here — the founder switches ventures through a ⌘O overlay, not a rail list, and one venture fills a session. Collapses to an icon strip; stage-maximal by default.
  • Center stage — the atlas. The infinite canvas, holding roughly 85% of the viewport at rest. Moves 2–4 all live here. The composer is the primary handle — chat-first steering — docked to the stage rather than floating over nodes.
  • Right inspector — context (docked, one panel). Opens on node selection: an effort's detail and drafts, an artifact, a teammate's current work (not a profile), or the run record behind any of it. Content swaps as selection changes; it never stacks a second overlay. This replaces the current overlapping right-side panels outright.
  • No separate activity console — the conversation is the console. The earlier plan had a bottom activity dock; the founder killed it. Agent activity and artifacts render inline in the conversation, where the founder is already looking. Two extensions (the canvas echoing conversation activity, and world-touching acts surfacing in-conversation) are the right direction but need careful design — tracked as open in §7.
  • The grid flexes with resolution. Rail, stage, and inspector are a CSS grid whose tracks respond to viewport width via container queries. The canvas re-fits inside its own cell — which is exactly the viewport-adaptation model below.
The work loop — settled 2026-07-15 Finished work lands in the conversation like a colleague handing it over, and review is dialogue, not buttons: the founder reads it and says what he thinks — there are no accept/reject controls and no review states to design. Real-world evidence comes back in through capabilities (Gmail, Exa, Apollo, and the rest are the ports for both outbound acts and returning evidence), and it attaches to context through those same ports. When work stalls it shows both ways — the effort node reads blocked and names what it needs, and the teammate asks in the conversation. Everything is fully transparent: any artifact or event links down to its complete record (§1's sixth noun). Work runs live while the founder is present; he can steer mid-flight by saying so in the conversation and running work adjusts, and an effort ends when he says so in conversation. (Only while the app is open — see "How Drover ships" in §1.)
Prior art LangSmith Studio — the closest analogue: left nav rail, center graph canvas, docked right inspector (Input / Output / Attributes), composer docked below the stage. Retool — the IDE triptych: component rail, center canvas, right inspector with a top command bar. The Letta ADE is the same family. Drover's version keeps the warm-paper aesthetic and puts the venture atlas, not a node-wiring graph, on the stage.
Move 2

Spatial integrity engine

The floor inside the stage. Nothing on the canvas scores above 3/10 without it.

With the frame in place, the atlas still has to place itself. Every remaining symptom in §2 traces to hand-authored coordinates that don't know about each other or about the stage cell they live in. Three mechanisms replace them:

  • Collision-free auto-layout, fully automatic. A layout engine (elkjs layered/force, or d3-force) computes node positions so cards physically cannot overlap. Layout re-runs on graph change, and Motion's layout animation glides cards to their new spots — they never teleport. The founder never drags a node: the engine owns placement entirely (founder-decided 2026-07-15), which removes hand-arranged coordinates as a source of collisions rather than merely tidying around them.
  • Stage-aware fit. fitView turns on, fitting the atlas to its stage cell — the space left after the rail and inspector take theirs — and re-fits whenever the rail collapses, the inspector opens, or the window resizes. This is the dynamic-resolution answer: the atlas composes itself for whatever stage cell it gets, on a 1280px laptop or a 5K display, instead of assuming 1920×1080.
  • An effort's drafts collapse into the effort. The seven cards clipped off the edge were an effort's drafts spilling onto the canvas. They collapse into a single stack chip on their parent effort ("7 drafts"), expanding only on focus or when the founder opens the effort in the inspector. (Drafts are part of an effort, not a separate concept — see the six-noun set in §1.)
  • Finished work sinks to a faded background layer. Completed efforts don't clutter the active atlas — they recede into a faded background layer (founder-decided), still there and expandable, but out of the way of what's live. This keeps the automatic layout working over a small live set instead of an ever-growing pile.
Move 3

Semantic altitude

The signature. Depth by zoom, not by clutter.

Overturned — read this first A later machinery check (2026-07-15) cut named Horizon / Working / Focus modes and any altitude switcher (see §7b and the build contract §3). What ships is zoom-responsive card rendering only: the same graph re-details continuously with React Flow's zoom value — no snap altitudes, no mode selector, no "you are at Horizon" chrome, and no per-viewport altitude default. The three named tiers below survive only as an intuition for how much a card reveals at a given zoom, not as navigable states. Selection-focus (expand one effort, recede the rest) and staged materialization are unaffected.

Once the atlas places itself on the stage, zoom becomes meaning. The same graph renders at more or less detail as the founder zooms, on React Flow's continuous zoom value. Depth is a feature — the founder chooses how close they stand.

Zoomed out

Horizon

The whole venture reads at a glance: crew, cluster chips in founder words ("7 efforts underway"), the gate marker, and the central "What should change?" hub. No card bodies.

Mid

Working

Full node cards, collision-free layout. This is where the founder reads the theory and directs work — the default altitude on a normal-sized screen.

Zoomed in

Focus

The selected line of work and its artifacts expand into rich detail. Everything else dims and shrinks — soft depth-of-field, not hidden. The founder never loses the map.

  • Zoom-responsive rendering, continuous — not fixed altitudes (corrected). The interview first chose ADE-conventional snap altitudes; a machinery check overturned that (§7b). Cards re-detail continuously with React Flow's zoom value — title/glyph at low zoom, body and footer as zoom rises — with no snap tiers, no switcher, and no altitude chrome. There is no camera-travel-between-modes to animate because there are no modes.
  • No per-viewport altitude default. The earlier "a 1280px screen opens at Horizon, a 4K screen at Working" rule is cut with the altitude modes. Resolution adaptation comes entirely from the stage-aware fit in Move 2 — the atlas composes itself for whatever stage cell it gets — not from an altitude system.
  • Chat is the handle, and materialization is a staged unfolding. Directing from the composer draws the theory in front of the founder over ~2–3 seconds — nodes born in sequence while the conversation narrates what's appearing (founder-decided), not a single instant snap. The signature moment made kinetic.
Partly scaffolded already The type system already carries the three altitudes — AtlasAltitude = "venture" | "architecture" | "detail" in atlasTypes.ts:13, and nodes already carry a focusRole of focus / related / context. The altitude concept is present; what's missing is the collision-free layout beneath it (Move 2) and the zoom-driven rendering and animation on top. This is elevation of an existing seam, not a new invention.
Move 4

Language and component rebuild

Runs across the whole shell. Every founder-facing surface, redesigned.

  • Seven founder-facing components, each with an anatomy spec and a full state grid: the two shell regions (the conversation rail, right inspector) and five node archetypes — teammate, effort (which carries its own drafts), capability, gate ("Nothing needs you yet"), and the central question hub. There is no separate "prepared work" component: drafts belong to an effort, per the six-noun set in §1. Detailed in §4.
  • Titles from content, never from IDs. A draft inside an effort is titled by what it is ("Draft: minimum buildable version"), with the staged-… identifier moved to metadata under a disclosure.
  • A copy pass that removes "bets", "drifting", and "lines" from the interface and replaces them with ordinary language. Detailed in §5.
  • Teammates as self-aware characters. The crew get faces and personality pushed further, not abstracted away (founder-decided) — the firm should feel staffed, not automated. Teammates are explicitly self-aware agents; personality surfaces at judgment moments. Clicking a teammate opens their current work in the standard inspector — there is no profile surface. The firm routes directions visibly: a teammate claims a direction in the conversation and says why. (How persona system prompts are written and structured is an open engineering task, tracked in §7 — not character fiction to design here.) Cut by a later machinery check: track records, blind-spot profiles, crew reviews, and hire proposals — the founder creates a new teammate directly when he wants one.
  • No degraded state to design. The red "Desktop host required" alarm and the whole snapshot dual-state are gone: Drover is a local Electron desktop app from now on (§1's "How Drover ships"), and the browser build survives only as a dev/test harness. There is no founder-facing degraded chrome.
  • Entry replaces the venture picker. The picker screen dies. The founder lands straight in their last venture; a ⌘O overlay switches ventures (one venture per session, ventures fully separate). Deferred instruments — the minimap and command palette — wait until the atlas is large enough to need them (§7).

07 — Exploration surface

What the founder interviews settled, and what's still open

A founder-interview series completed on 2026-07-15 across five areas — the ADE frame, the crew, the work loop, the gate and trust, and how Drover ships. It resolved most of the option spaces this document had left open — several of them in ways that overturn earlier assumptions (the separate activity console is gone; the venture-picker screen dies; the browser mode dies as a founder surface). Just as important, several decisions the interviews first reached were later overturned by machinery checks — asking what the mechanism actually needs to be — and the decision log marks those "cut by machinery check" so the final state is unambiguous. The decisions are folded through the whole document; this section records the option spaces and keeps the ones that genuinely need mockup rounds or engineering to settle. The decision log at the end is the durable record.

a · ADE layout weighting

decided · stage-maximal

How much room the chrome gets by default.

Stage-maximal ✓

The left rail collapses to an icon strip; the canvas holds roughly 85% of the viewport. Chrome earns its space only on demand. This is the closest expression of "the canvas is the star."

Rail-heavy

A wide always-present rail. Rejected: it standing-taxes the stage the founder came for.

Console-forward

A tall bottom run-stream. Moot — the separate console was killed entirely (see d).

b · Altitude model

overturned · zoom-responsive rendering only

The shape depth-by-zoom takes. Overturned by a later machinery check (2026-07-15): named Horizon / Working / Focus modes and any altitude switcher are cut. What ships is zoom-responsive card rendering only — cards simplify as the canvas zooms out and re-detail as it zooms in, on React Flow's continuous zoom value; there is no mode, no switcher, and no per-altitude viewport default. Selection-focus (expand one effort, recede the rest) survives as selection behavior, not a mode. This is the authoritative state — the build contract's §3 correction governs, and the "three fixed altitudes" prose in Move 3 and Phase 3 below is superseded by it.

Three fixed altitudes

ADE-conventional: predictable snap altitudes (Horizon / Working / Focus) with smooth camera travel. First chosen by deferring to convention; cut by machinery check — modes and a switcher were more machinery than the surface needs. Zoom-responsive rendering delivers the depth without them.

Continuous zoom LOD

Map-like continuous detail. Rejected: harder to keep legible at every intermediate zoom, and less predictable.

Focus-mode only

One flat altitude plus focus. Rejected: loses the zoomed-out read that makes the whole firm legible from afar.

c · Node card density

resolved by mockup rounds

Explicitly left open — the founder wants two to three real treatments to react to on sight, not a decision on paper. Mockup round 1 should render these against real Drover data.

Rich cards

Title, body preview, status, crew faces — everything at rest. Most legible without interaction; densest canvas.

Title-plus-state

Title, a status dot, and a state word; detail appears on selection. Calmer canvas; one more step to read detail.

Glyph-first

Near-iconographic at rest — shape and color carry meaning, text appears only close in. Most atlas-like from afar; demands a legible visual language.

d · Where live agent work shows

decided · chat is the console

Originally a separate bottom dock. The interview killed that surface.

The conversation is the console ✓

No separate activity surface. Agent activity and artifacts render inline in the one conversation — the crew shows what it's doing and hands work back in place. Two extensions are promising but need careful design (below): the canvas echoing that activity, and world-touching acts appearing in-conversation.

Separate dock — stream / timeline / lanes

All three rejected. A second live surface competes with chat for the founder's attention and splits "what's happening" across two places.

e · Visual temperature

decided · warm paper stays

Whether to keep the warm paper or move to dark instrument chrome.

Warm paper ✓

Kept. No dark chrome. The ink-on-paper identity is Drover's strongest visual asset and the thing no other ADE has — the taste references (Linear, Figma/FigJam, Cursor) are for their polish and spatial freedom, not their color.

Darker instrument · Adaptive

Both rejected. Dark chrome forfeits the identity; adaptive doubles the surface to keep coherent and dilutes it.

f · Navigation instruments

resolved by mockup rounds

Deferred, deliberately: minimap, command palette, and other wayfinding wait until the atlas is large enough to actually need them. Building them before the atlas has scale is guessing at a problem that isn't here yet. Revisit when a real venture's atlas outgrows a single screen.

g · Conversation rendering detail

resolved by mockup rounds

The conversation-is-the-console decision makes one detail load-bearing and unsettled: how inline activity and artifacts render without noise. A teammate claiming a direction, work landing back like a colleague handing it over, a blocked effort asking for what it needs, an artifact linking down to its record — all of this in one conversation. Done clumsily it turns the calm surface into a firehose. It also opens the canvas echoing that activity (nodes lighting up as work lands) and world-touching acts surfacing in-conversation. All of it needs real treatments against real data before it's built.

h · Persona system-prompt architecture

engineering task

Deepening the crew (Yara, Mira, Soren, Kai) as self-aware characters with lenses, not limits, raises a concrete engineering question: how the persona system prompts are actually written and structured so a teammate holds a consistent voice, defers to a sharper lens, and surfaces disagreement only when it forks the work. This is prompt engineering, not character fiction to draw here — flagged so it isn't mistaken for a design decision.

i · Idea-stage first work

resolved by mockup rounds

A venture can start from a description, not only from a repo (see first run below). When there's no product to read yet, the crew's first work has to make the idea concrete — but what that first move looks like, and how the first canvas fills for an idea-stage venture, is unsettled and needs mockup rounds.

08 — Build plan

Phased, each phase a shippable slice

Phase numbering — the contract governs This section's phase grouping predates the engineering build contract and its numbers do not map one-to-one to it. ade-build-contract.md §6 is the authoritative phase list a build fleet follows (Night 1: P1 shell+layout · P2 language+components · P3 zoom-responsive rendering · P4 routing + review-dialogue + trust-grants; Night 2: P5 streaming+steering · P6 evidence · P7 first-run · P8 one-crew · P9 Electron). Read the phases below for intent; take sequencing, scope, and acceptance from the contract.
Who builds this — founder-stated 2026-07-15 The ADE shell and layout engine are built by Opus agents, not Codex — a division-of-labor change from the earlier plan. The phases below name what "done" means; the first one is the founder-named first build.

Each phase ships something a founder can use. Phase 1 is the ADE shell plus the layout engine together — the founder named that pairing as the first build, and neither half stands without the other: the shell has nowhere to put a colliding atlas, and the engine has no stage to fit into.

Phase 1

ADE shell + layout engine — the floor (Opus agents)

Build the docked workbench and the collision-free atlas as one slice, inside the local Electron shell (the browser build stays a dev/test harness). The shell: a CSS-grid frame with a left rail that holds the one conversation with the firm (a single conversation, not a thread list — it splits only when volume hurts), a center-stage canvas with the composer docked to it, and a contextual right inspector that swaps content on selection. Stage-maximal by default — the rail collapses to an icon strip and the canvas holds ~85% of the viewport. No separate activity dock: agent activity and artifacts render inline in the conversation. The engine: spike elkjs vs d3-force on the real projection, wire the winner to feed React Flow positions, turn on fitView fitting the stage cell, and animate layout changes with Motion so cards glide. Placement is fully automatic — the founder never drags.

Done when Panels never stack or bleed: the rail docks and collapses, the inspector is one swapping panel. No two nodes overlap at 1280 / 1440 / 1920 / 2560 wide, in any shell configuration. Opening or collapsing a region re-fits the atlas to its stage cell within one animation-frame budget. The composer is docked to the stage. Mockup round 1 renders this whole frame in three divergent takes against the real Drover venture data.
Phase 2

Language and component pass

Retitle an effort's drafts from content; move IDs to disclosure. Ship the seven components — the shell regions (conversation rail, inspector) and the five node archetypes (teammate, effort, capability, gate, question hub) — with full state grids, including teammates as self-aware characters with faces (clicking one opens their current work, no profile) and finished work sunk to the faded background layer. Kill the leaked nouns in copy and add the founder-language build guard. The degraded web state is gone entirely (Electron desktop-only), so there is no snapshot chrome to design.

Done when No founder-facing surface shows staged-… IDs, "bets", "drifting", or "lines"; nothing implies a seventh concept beyond the six nouns. The language guard passes in CI. Every component renders all its grid states. Completed efforts sink to the faded background layer and expand on demand.
Phase 3

Zoom-responsive rendering

(Was "Semantic altitude"; named modes and the switcher were cut by a machinery check — §7b, build contract §3. This is the authoritative Phase 3 for a build fleet; the build contract's phase list governs sequencing.) Render card detail continuously from the one graph via React Flow's zoom value — title/glyph at low zoom, body and footer as zoom rises — with no named altitudes, no switcher, and no per-viewport altitude default. Selection-focus dims and shrinks the unfocused world when an effort is selected; composer direction materializes as a staged unfolding (~2–3s, nodes born in sequence while the conversation narrates).

Done when Zooming out simplifies cards to title/glyph with no mode chrome anywhere; zooming in re-details them; selecting an effort expands it while the rest recedes without disappearing; typing a direction unfolds the theory node-by-node in front of the founder.
Phase 4

The bidirectional handle and the switcher

Wire the conversation and canvas to focus each other — conversation entries and nodes fly to each other on selection. Add composer @-node references, file/image drops, and voice input (no slash verbs). Replace the dead venture-picker screen with entry straight into the last venture plus a ⌘O switcher overlay. Build the minimal gate: a world-touching act waits, the inspector shows the exact payload (the email as the recipient sees it, the spend, the diff), and the founder answers in the conversation. Trust is remembered dialogue — when the founder says "you can send these yourself now," the firm remembers and stops waiting on that; there is no policy system, no caps UI, no delay windows, no undo queue. The run record is the paper trail.

Done when Selecting a conversation entry frames its node and vice versa; @-referencing a node in the composer targets it; ⌘O switches ventures without a picker screen; a waiting act shows its exact payload in the inspector; and once the founder tells the firm in conversation it may send a kind of act itself, that kind stops waiting — remembered from what he said, not configured in a settings panel.
Phase 5

Deferred — navigation instruments

Minimap, command-palette navigation, and other wayfinding wait until a real venture's atlas is large enough to need them (decided in §7). Named here so the sequence is honest, not scheduled.

Trigger, not a date Build when a real atlas outgrows a single screen and the founder reaches for a way to navigate it. Not before.

09 — What remains unproven

Open risks, stated plainly

This direction is committed, but several things are genuinely unproven and one whole flow is unbuilt. Naming them is the point.

The ADE shell could make Drover feel like software to administer

An IDE frame is the right structural answer to panel anarchy, but it carries a real tension with Drover's ethos: the canvas is meant to be the star and chat the easy handle, and the spec's fatal mode #4 is flow-killing — "friction, setup ceremony, mental-state drag." A rail and an inspector can read as a cockpit to operate rather than a venture to grow. The founder's interview decisions push hard against this — stage-maximal by default (rail collapses to icons, canvas ~85%), chat-first steering, and no separate activity console — so the shell starts as recessed as it can. Whether that's recessed enough, or the frame still reads as a control panel, is a taste call only real use settles.

Discriminating test: a founder does a full session — direct, review, release — touching only the composer and the stage, never needing to "manage" the shell. If they feel they're operating an IDE, the chrome is too loud and should recede further by default.

Zoom disorientation

Zoom-as-meaning is powerful and can also make a founder lose their place — "where am I, and how do I get back?" With named altitude modes and the switcher cut (§7b), the surface is continuous zoom-responsive rendering, which avoids the "three disorienting screens" failure but keeps its own version of the risk: as detail continuously appears and recedes, the founder can still lose their bearings. Depth-of-field on selection keeps the map visible when focused. But the usual wayfinding backstop, the minimap, is deliberately deferred until the atlas is large enough to need it — so early on, disorientation has fewer safety nets. Whether continuous zoom reads as one legible world is a real-use question, and it interacts directly with the "I can see everything" success bar.

Discriminating test: a founder zooms out, then back to the work they were on, without reaching for a way to navigate. If they can't, either the zoom response is too abrupt or the minimap's deferral was premature.

elk layout stability on a live-updating graph

Auto-layout is clean on a static graph. Drover's graph changes as work returns and the crew acts — and because placement is now fully automatic (the founder never drags), there are no pinned founder coordinates to anchor the layout. That removes hand-arranged collisions but raises the stakes on stability: if elk re-solves the whole layout on every change, the whole atlas can reshuffle under the founder even with animation. Incremental layout — re-solving only the changed neighborhood and letting finished work sink to the faded background layer to keep the live set small — is the likely answer, but its stability under live updates is unverified.

Discriminating test: a single new effort appears and only its neighborhood adjusts; the rest of the atlas holds still.

The signature flow itself is not built

Per docs/STATE.md (2026-07-15), the immediate-working-theory flow and the founder-language migration are explicitly not built. Current production still uses internal nouns in several surfaces and still treats whole-system assembly as something the founder approves before useful work. This document designs toward that flow; it does not describe a shipped one. The atlas canvas (React Flow, pan/zoom/placement, altitude type-scaffolding) is built — the collision-free layout, viewport fit, and altitude rendering on top of it are not.

What would settle it: a real founder types one plain sentence and gets a coherent, non-colliding, plain-language theory that changes what the firm works on next — the alpha gate the spec names, which no deterministic test stands in for.

Does the atlas stay legible with no grooming at all?

The founder decided placement is fully automatic — he never drags a node. That is the right call for flow, but it raises the stakes on the engine: with no manual grooming as an escape hatch, if the automatic layout is ever hard to read, the founder has no way to fix it by hand. Legibility rests entirely on the engine plus the faded background layer keeping the live set small. This is the spec's own reconsider-condition made sharper, and it collides directly with the "I can see everything" success bar: an auto-layout the founder can't correct must be right on its own.

Discriminating test: a founder returns after a week of ambient work to an atlas they never touched, and it reads at a glance — no cleanup available and none needed.

Operator types beyond the solo founder are unbuilt and unmodeled

The collaborator (can answer the acts that are waiting) and the viewer (read-only atlas walk) from §1 are a design constraint, not a shipped capability. The founder-wall's host-issued release capability already models releasing an act as a permission boundary, which is the right seam to build on — but no second operator identity, no read-only atlas mode, and no shared-venture access exists today. The risk is designing the shell as if the operator is always the owner and having to unpick that later.

What keeps it cheap now: honor the existing wall capability as the permission boundary rather than inventing a UI-only gate, and keep the atlas's mutation paths separable from its pan/zoom/altitude paths so a read-only mode is a flag, not a rewrite. Neither requires building the operator types — only not precluding them.

Only-while-open trades away the "runs around the clock" promise

The production decision that work runs only while the app is open — no background service, no cloud runner — is a deliberate, honest simplification, but it collides with Drover's headline promise that a permanent AI crew works "around the clock." In practice "since you left" means "since your last session," not work done overnight while the machine slept. The crew's presence and the sense of a firm that keeps working have to survive that constraint, and the copy must never imply progress that didn't happen.

Discriminating test: a founder closes the app mid-effort and reopens it the next day; the effort resumes cleanly from where it paused, and nothing in the surface claims work happened while it was closed.
